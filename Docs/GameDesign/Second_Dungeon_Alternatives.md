# Second dungeon — ten alternative signature mechanics

Date: 2026-10-31. Status: unselected proposals, not implemented capabilities.
User rejected furnace circuits, resonant cores, borrowed enemy techniques and
risk-for-power relics. Do not restore them as prerequisites. Visual identity is
open; keep all narrative suggestions provisional. Desired level band: 10–20.

The key criterion is a new enjoyable player action or consequence, beyond
Mortfall's existing movement/target-priority pressure. Costs below are relative
engineering/content scope, not time estimates. None has passed a playtest.

## 1. Spatial gates

Paired visible gates transport actors and ordinary projectiles. The player can
cross a formation, flank a guard and route a shot from a different direction.
Example: approach a defender from the front, pass through a nearby side gate,
then strike its exposed rear before it turns. No compulsory timed portal puzzle.
Visuals: fractured observatory, folded halls, stone portals with clear pairs.
Cost: high. Start with one pair, player/enemy traversal and one projectile type;
resolve exit clearance, facing, cooldown against loops and camera visibility.
Ordinary melee benefits through traversal rather than bespoke remote hitboxes.
Fail if gates are only a faster door or produce unpredictable forced teleporting.

## 2. Delayed allied echo

A visible companion echo repeats the player's ordinary attack and recent movement
after a short fixed delay. The player can strike, reposition and set up a second
attack from the previous position. This is a small tactical duet with one's own
actions, not borrowed enemy powers or simply a damage-stat buff.
Visuals: archive of memories, chronal crypt, frozen gestures in reliefs.
Cost: very high. Prototype only a basic attack and a bounded local movement replay;
no promise to replay every recipe, blink, summon or channel. No recursive echoes
or on-hit farming. Fail if the optimal play is unchanged and it is just free DPS.

## 3. Enemy attacks hurt their own formation

Selected committed heavy attacks can damage or stagger other enemies. The player
baits a charge or slam through a group, then exploits the resulting opening.
Example: sidestep a charging brute so it crashes into its ranged support.
Visuals: beast pits, ruined war arena, feral guard barracks.
Cost: medium. Limit friendly fire to readable attacks, use attack identity and
bounded interruption, preserve normal attacks' value and avoid universal enemy
stunlock. Enemy placement makes the opportunity legible. Fail if passive kiting
is always superior to attacking. This is active manipulation of an opponent's
commitment, not another stationary hazard source.

## 4. Liberate a small allied group

Free imprisoned creatures or bound guardians during fights. The freed ally stays
for subsequent rooms, within a small party cap; distinguish a durable frontliner
from a ranged supporter. The player chooses whom to rescue and protects allies
without any compulsory escort objective or failure on ally death.
Visuals: prison fortress, binding sanctuary, abandoned menagerie.
Cost: high: factions, ally targeting, cross-room lifetime, pathing and UI. Prototype
one ally in one room before persistence. No new loot resource or command wheel
required initially. Fail if allies erase combat or become repetitive babysitting.

## 5. Dismantle large constructs

Some substantial regular enemies have visible, independently breakable parts.
Breaking a weapon arm removes one attack; breaking the leg brace exposes the body.
Choice of attack position changes the fight, with satisfying tangible destruction.
Visuals: reliquary of animated armor, fallen stone guardians, giant workshop.
Cost: high. Start with one large enemy and two real part hitboxes; plan damage
sharing and attack cancellation so AoE is viable without trivializing all parts.
Not every fodder enemy needs anatomy. Fail if parts are merely mandatory extra HP
bars or require precision beyond the game's targeting controls.

## 6. Two overlapping versions of a room

At readable local anchors the player switches between material and spectral states
of the same room. Some enemies occupy only one state; the player can split a pack,
escape a projectile and choose which subgroup to engage next. Travel geometry is
identical for the first prototype; no hidden floor disappearance.
Visuals: haunted palace, sunken monastery with its intact memory visible beneath.
Cost: very high: actor/projectile ownership, state persistence, transition rules
and simultaneous-state readability. A switch is a deliberate local action with
re-entry limits, not constant invulnerability. Fail if optimal play is switching
after every hit or repeatedly searching for the one world where a target is hittable.

## 7. Selectable gravity direction

Room anchors switch the player between floor and ceiling traversal; a subset of
enemies can inhabit both surfaces. Attacks, jumps and evasion gain genuinely new
spatial combinations. Example: switch to the ceiling to attack a gallery through
an opening while avoiding the formation below.
Visuals: inverted cathedral, celestial engine, floating rock vaults.
Cost: very high: locomotion, grounding, one-way platforms, camera, every relevant
skill and enemy traversal. Prototype one reversible room with standard attacks.
Fail if movement feels worse or it is a traversal gimmick with no combat choices.

## 8. Player-controlled arena configuration

One readable mechanism changes a limited local arrangement: a bridge raises into
cover, a partition opens a flank, or an existing safe platform shifts between two
stable positions. The player deliberately reshapes a fight rather than waiting
for a moving-floor schedule. No crushing/instant-death requirement.
Visuals: clockwork fortress, stone sluices, transformable defensive chambers.
Cost: high. Prototype one two-state bridge; maintain safe occupied-space handling
and enemy navigation. Avoid a universal runtime room generator. Fail if every
lever has one obvious correct state or interactions only interrupt combat flow.

## 9. Battle on a moving procession

Combat takes place across a few broad moving carriers with stable relative routes.
The player changes carrier to reach a ranged enemy, isolate a pursuer or set up a
wide attack; enemies cross with clear commitments. Movement itself forms a
readable, enjoyable stage for fights rather than an auto-scrolling death timer.
Visuals: chained funerary barges, a floating relic convoy, colossal stone lifts.
Cost: very high: moving-platform physics, combat origins, camera, transit and
enemy navigation. Prototype two platforms and a melee/ranged pair; provide recovery
from falling. Fail if the background merely moves or precision platforming
overwhelms the ordinary action-RPG combat.

## 10. Two authored character scales

At clear transitions the player becomes miniature or returns to normal. A miniature
route enters a giant guardian's casing or passes beneath its defense; returning
to normal gives a different attack opportunity. Keep equipment/skill identity,
with explicitly authored interactions at both scales.
Visuals: titan's reliquary, alchemical palace, interiors of enormous relic machinery.
Cost: very high: collision, reach, damage/area interpretation, pixel scale, camera
and two-scale encounter authoring. Start with two fixed scales in one room, not a
continuous scaling system. Fail if novelty is only visual or one scale dominates
all fights. Do not promise that scaling a Transform implements the mechanic.

## Selection and smallest proof

Most are real new systems, not low-cost variants of current skills. Candidates 3
and 5 give the clearest direct combat payoff with less global movement disruption;
8 offers arena agency; 1/2/6/7/9/10 require broader feasibility work. This is a scope
comparison, not approval or a claim of superior fun.

After user shortlisting, compare two concepts in small graybox encounters. Measure
whether the new interaction is voluntarily used, understandable at native size,
and enjoyable across melee/ranged/spell builds. Do not author a full biome or
entire roster until that interaction earns its place. Difficulty comes afterward
through meaningful enemy combinations and tuned values, not a tenfold stat jump.
